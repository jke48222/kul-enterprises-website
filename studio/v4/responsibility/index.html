003 / The operating standard

Five clauses.
None of them optional.

Safety isn’t a slogan here. It’s the obligation that arrived with the authority.

A

Maintenance

The machine earns the road

The tractor is inspected before every trip — brakes, tires, lights, fluids, coupling — and anything found gets addressed before the load does. Preventive work is scheduled rather than deferred, because a well-kept truck is the first safety device on board.

Problems get fixed before they get famous. A defect noticed in the yard costs an afternoon. The same defect noticed on an interstate costs considerably more, and not always in money.

The rule — If it isn’t right, it doesn’t roll.

49 CFR 396

B

Hours of service

Rested, legal, logged

Hours-of-service rules exist because tired drivers hurt people. Hours are logged straight, rest is taken when it is due, and no rate is worth pushing a clock.

If a delivery window cannot be met legally, you hear it before the load is booked, not after the clock has run out. We re-plan the run or we decline it. That conversation is part of the service, not a failure of it.

The rule — The clock is never negotiable.

49 CFR 395

C

Communication

24 / 7 · a person answers

Silence is where trust dies. Dispatch is reachable around the clock, and when something changes on the road — weather, traffic, a dock backing up — you hear it from us first, with a revised plan attached.

Check calls get answered. ETAs stay honest. Bad news travels as fast as good news, because the alternative is a receiver finding out before you do.

The rule — You will never wonder where your freight is.

678-972-1148

D

Cargo

Your freight, our name

From the moment the seal goes on, the cargo rides under our name. Securement is checked before the wheels turn, seals are verified and documented, and the paperwork is clean at both ends of the run so the count on the bill matches the count on the floor.

We treat every trailer as though the contents belong to someone we answer to — because they do.

The rule — It arrives the way it left.

49 CFR 393

E

Declination

The load we don’t take

Not every load should be run, and an honest carrier says so before the trailer is hooked rather than after. We turn down freight we cannot secure properly, windows we cannot cover legally, and equipment we are not satisfied with.

Declining costs us a load. Running it wrong would cost a great deal more, and not only to us. A new authority has no history to coast on — which is precisely why this clause is written down alongside the other four.

The rule — We’d rather lose the load than run it wrong.

005 / The pre-trip walk

What gets checked
before the wheels turn

The same walk, every trip, in the same order. It takes as long as it takes.

Clause A · in practice

[ 01 ]Brakes & air lines Slack adjusters, drums, lines and couplings, and the air loss test.
[ 02 ]Tires, wheels & rims Tread, pressure, sidewalls, lugs and any sign of a hub running warm.
[ 03 ]Lights & reflectors Every lamp walked and seen lit, trailer included, before dark or after it.
[ 04 ]Coupling & fifth wheel Jaws locked, pin seated, apron flat, and a tug test that actually pulls.
[ 05 ]Steering & suspension Play at the wheel, springs, hangers, airbags and shocks, both sides.
[ 06 ]Fluids & leaks Levels topped, and the ground under the truck read for what dripped onto it.
[ 07 ]Glass, mirrors & wipers Clean, set and undamaged — a mirror you cannot see out of is a defect.
[ 08 ]Securement & seal Straps, blocking and doors checked, seal number verified and written down.

▸ Why there is no safety score on this page

Carriers usually put a number here. We are not going to. KUL holds a new authority, which means there is no long roadside record to publish and any short one, dressed up as a statistic, would be the exact opposite of the point of this chapter. What we can show you is the walk itself, the standard it belongs to, and the registrations that let you go and check the record for yourself as it builds.

006 / Federal parts

The rules we run under

These are not certifications and we are not claiming them as awards. They are the parts of the federal code a for-hire motor carrier is bound by — the standard we’re held to whether or not anybody is watching.

49 CFR391

Qualification of drivers

Licence, medical certificate and driving record kept current and on file — the driver is part of the equipment check.

49 CFR392

Driving of commercial motor vehicles

How the truck is operated: no driving ill or fatigued, no rolling on a condition that should have been fixed in the yard.

49 CFR393

Parts, accessories & securement

Equipment condition and cargo securement standards — the code behind clause D and behind items 04 and 08 of the walk.

49 CFR395

Hours of service of drivers

The clock. Logged straight, rest taken when it is due, and a window re-planned rather than driven through.

49 CFR396

Inspection, repair & maintenance

Pre-trip and post-trip inspection and systematic maintenance — what clause A puts into practice every single morning.

49 CFR382

Controlled substances & alcohol testing

Participation in a testing programme as required of every carrier holding interstate authority. No carve-outs for small fleets.

49 CFR387

Minimum financial responsibility

The insurance floor a for-hire carrier must hold to keep its authority — the reason “licensed and insured” is a condition, not a boast.

Federal code · verifiable independently of anything we say
